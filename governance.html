    About

    Design and governance

    Updated 5 sections on this page are new or updated since 1.2.0-rc.1 (2026-11-06). Everything that changed

    How the profile is governed and changed, the decisions behind it, what is known to be wrong or missing, how it is checked, and an honest self-assessment.

    StatusUpdated

    Proposed. Not endorsed. To become a standard it needs, in order: review by the Metadata Implementation Working Group, a decision by the owning team in the Government Data directorate of the Government Digital Service, and endorsement by the Data Standards Authority Steering Board, as the xGov MEM had. Until then the xGov MEM remains the endorsed model.

    Checkpoint

    Intent
    Evolve the xGov MEM into DCAT-AP-UK, resolve its backlog, align to DCAT 3, DCAT-AP 3.0.1, the 2026 guidance and the future-state strategy, and present it in a portal that joins the estate.
    Arc
    • Read the xGov MEM source, its 99 issues and pull requests, the 2026 guidance, the Data Asset Management Policy, the Data Marketplace schemas and the future data architecture.
    • Wrote the profile as one YAML source, with codelists, lineage and worked examples.
    • Generated SHACL, JSON Schema, JSON-LD context, LinkML, SKOS and CSV; built the browser validator; held all three validators to the same verdict on every example and deliberate break.
    • Built the portal and its knowledge layer, compared the four portals like for like, and joined the estate compass as its fourth bearing.
    • 1.1: scanned DCAT profiles in Europe, the US and sectors; added nine sector extensions, cross-border flows, a quality score and levels, AI-ready and quantum-ready features, an adoption toolkit, bulk and OpenAPI converters and an MCP server; linked the future pan-UK metadata architecture.
    Verdict
    Meets the brief as a proposal. Needs user research, specialist review of the K mappings and the governance steps before it can be a standard.

    Versioning and releases

    • Semantic versioning. A major version changes an obligation from optional or recommended to mandatory, removes a property or closes a codelist value. A minor version adds a property, a codelist value or a recommendation. A patch fixes wording, examples or guidance.
    • Each release publishes the shapes, schema, context, codelists and examples as release assets, with notes, as issue 70 asked.
    • The previous release stays published, so records can say which version they conform to.

    Changing the profile

    1. Ask: Anyone raises a change request with the form. It asks for the user need, the evidence and the impact on existing records.
    2. Assess: The maintainers label it, check it against DCAT, DCAT-AP and the backlog, and say whether it is a major, minor or patch change.
    3. Discuss: Major and minor changes go to the Metadata Implementation Working Group for review.
    4. Decide: The owning team decides. Major changes go to the Data Standards Authority for endorsement.
    5. Release: The change is made in profile/dcat-ap-uk.yaml with a test, passes the release gate and ships in the next release.

    DecisionsUpdated

    ADR 0001: A new repository, not a rename Accepted

    Context
    Issue 78 asked whether to rename the xGov MEM repository. Its discussion said a completely new solution suits a new repository.
    Decision
    Start a new repository (first cyberdudeuk/dcat-uk-ap, renamed cyberdudeuk/dcat-ap-uk on 6 November 2026; GitHub redirects the old name). Keep every xGov MEM slot in the crosswalk. Propose archiving the old repository with a pointer, once the owners agree.
    Consequences
    Old issue links keep working. Records written to the xGov MEM convert with the validator.

    ADR 0002: Generate from one YAML file with our own script, not LinkML Accepted

    Context
    The xGov MEM used LinkML and MkDocs. Ten of its twelve open pull requests were dependency updates to that toolchain, and a LinkML limitation blocked issue 63.
    Decision
    One profile file; a small Python generator for SHACL, JSON Schema, JSON-LD context, SKOS and the portal. A LinkML file is still generated for continuity.
    Consequences
    Fewer dependencies to patch. CI checks the generated LinkML file with LinkML's own validator and linter, so it cannot drift from the other validators (D-029).

    ADR 0003: Contact form preferred over email in contact points Accepted

    Context
    Issue 71 found the contact point was defined as an email address. Email addresses go stale when people move, and can be personal data.
    Decision
    A contact point gives a contact form, a team mailbox or both, and never a named person. The form is preferred. The portal publishes no email address at all.
    Consequences
    Existing records with team mailboxes still conform. Records with personal addresses should be changed.

    ADR 0004: Obligations for critical data assets as conditionals Accepted

    Context
    The April 2026 guidance asks for more for critical data assets than for other data.
    Decision
    A critical data asset flag switches on extra mandatory properties, in the same record, rather than a second profile.
    Consequences
    One record serves the catalogue and the return. SHACL and JSON Schema both express the rule.

    ADR 0005: EU frequency list and data.gov.uk themes Accepted

    Context
    DCAT-AP uses the EU frequency vocabulary; the xGov MEM used Dublin Core terms. Issue 49 proposed the data.gov.uk topics for themes.
    Decision
    Use the EU frequency list, converting Dublin Core values. Use the 14 data.gov.uk topics for themes, mapped to EU data themes.
    Consequences
    UK records harvest into European tools. The mappings are the author's and need review (open register O-04).

    ADR 0007: Sector extensions that only add or raise Accepted

    Context
    Sectors need more than the base (health, geospatial, statistics, transport, AI, data spaces, energy). DCAT-AP solves this with separate extension profiles; the future pan-UK architecture plans domain profiles too.
    Decision
    Extensions in one file, merged onto the base at build time. An extension may raise an obligation, narrow a cardinality, add a pattern, a property or a class; the build refuses one that weakens the base. Each gets its own shapes and schema.
    Consequences
    A record that conforms to an extension conforms to the base. Sector terms from other namespaces are cited, not copied; terms this profile adds stay provisional (O-13).

    ADR 0008: Cross-border flows as two properties and a rule Accepted

    Context
    Data moves to the Crown Dependencies, Overseas Territories, Commonwealth and partners. UK GDPR restricts personal data leaving the UK; the ICO updated its guidance on 15 January 2026.
    Decision
    Record every place the data is held or sent, and the transfer safeguard. Rule R-08 requires the safeguard when personal data leaves the UK. The jurisdiction list is open, in ISO 3166 form.
    Consequences
    The record states the basis; it is not legal advice and not a transfer risk assessment. The adequacy column must be kept current (O-15).

    ADR 0009: One validator implementation, used everywhere Accepted

    Context
    The quality score, levels, converters and exports could be written twice, in Python for the build and JavaScript for the browser, and drift.
    Decision
    They live once, in site_src/validator.js. The build, the MCP server and the browser all run it; Python checks only what SHACL and JSON Schema check.
    Consequences
    Building needs Node. Tests compare the browser Turtle with the build RDF so the two paths cannot drift.

    ADR 0010: Quantum-ready means ready to migrate Accepted

    Context
    Post-quantum migration is a long programme (NCSC: discovery by 2028, completion by 2035). Metadata cannot make data quantum-safe.
    Decision
    Record algorithms as identifiers, warn on weak checksums, and record connection security on data services so the catalogue helps the inventory.
    Consequences
    The pages say plainly what the profile does not do.

    ADR 0015: A requirements stream for a family of profiles, with controlled layers kept out of the public repository Proposed

    Context
    The same needs keep coming up in engagement with catalogue owners, data asset management, discovery services, defence and security users, policing and geospatial teams: a common core, specialist layers, separate handling for records and data, explicit missing values, strict machine validation and honest mappings. Some of those needs can only be specified with material that is not public, and publishing who asked what would aggregate information about protected communities.
    Decision
    Keep one public requirements catalogue that names kinds of community only, labels each requirement with its evidence (engagement, verified public source or operating rule) and traces it to properties, rules and tests. Build the open core and its extension points here; list requirements needing controlled material with status Controlled and specify them elsewhere. Add record-level classification and explicit missing values to the core now.
    Consequences
    The requirements page counts how many are met, partly met, planned or controlled. C-22 fails the build on any protective marking or caveat, and a test limits the catalogue to kinds of community. No conformance claim is made for any allied or controlled specification.

    ADR 0016: Share the build, change-flag and self-check patterns of the connected solutions Accepted

    Context
    DCAT-AP-UK links to and is linked from the Data Quality Commons, the DAM capability playbooks, the GitHub Repos Asset Catalogue and the Knowledge Web. They share one way of building a portal (generated pages, a retractable drawer, display options, a colophon, checks with deliberate breaks) but had drifted: only the Data Quality Commons flagged what changed, only the DAM playbooks published a status page and only the catalogue ran a repository self-assessment. A reader moving between them met different conventions.
    Decision
    Adopt the three missing patterns. Change flags as the Data Quality Commons does them: a section baseline recorded at each release (tools/section_baseline.py), with New and Updated flags on drawer items and groups, on section headings, under page titles and in the global bar, and a list on What's new. A Status page as the DAM playbooks build it, fed by a health run on every build. The five self-* routines (verification, assessment, staleness, reporting, repair) as the catalogue reports them. Not adopted: GitHub Discussions voting and the ask-a-question assistant, which need the owning team to decide on moderation and hosting first.
    Consequences
    C-24 fails the build if a flag disagrees with downloads/changes-since.json; status.json and changes-since.json are machine-readable. Someone runs section_baseline.py at each release so flags reset; until then they accumulate. Flags count words only, so restyling never flags a section.

    ADR 0017: Routines do the upkeep; a person keeps control Accepted

    Context
    Keeping the portal right took a person for chores: resetting the change flags at each release, refreshing the organisation list after machinery of government changes, watching sources, chasing issues and reading many separate signals. Chores that rely on someone remembering get missed, as D-031 and D-032 showed. The same is true across the connected portals.
    Decision
    Every workflow is declared in ops/automation.yaml with a tier: 0 observe, 1 maintain (changes derived or reference data on its own, only after every check passes, in one commit naming the routine), 2 propose (a pull request or issue for a person) and 3 person only (releases, settings, deletions, anything sent outside the repository, claims). tools/routine.py runs every tier 1 routine the same way. The change flags reset straight after each release; upkeep runs weekly; triage labels waiting issues; a weekly digest issue is the owner's one place to look. A repository variable, AUTOMATION=off, stops every tier 1 change. Across the estate, a weekly conductor reads each repository's digest and status, writes an estate digest and opens pull requests for a person; it never merges.
    Consequences
    A person presses one button to release and reads one digest a week. tests/test_automation.py fails if a workflow is undeclared, a tier 1 routine ignores the kill switch, an action is not pinned to a commit or permissions are not declared. Routine commits are signed off with a Routine trailer, so the digest lists them and each is one revert to undo.

    ADR 0018: Run the estate as flight operations: test flights with an envelope, near misses reported without blame Accepted

    Context
    ADR 0017 made routines do the upkeep, but said nothing about trying new things safely or learning from what nearly went wrong. A conductor metaphor describes coordination, not safety or experiment. The owner asked for a model that trims waste, prevents incidents and pre-empts threats while still allowing frontier work inside guardrails with kill switches.
    Decision
    The estate is run as flight operations, after aviation safety practice: routines are flights inside an envelope (the tiers), the digest and Status page are the instruments, a weekly AI agent is operations control (dispatch and watch, never fly), and a person is pilot in command. Threats are named before they bite, after the Threat and Error Management framework (threats, errors, undesired states). New ideas fly as trials in ops/automation.yaml: each states a hypothesis, an envelope, a measure, an abort rule and an end date at most 90 days away, may only observe or propose, and has its own kill switch (TRIAL_ plus its name) as well as AUTOMATION. Near misses are reported without blame as occurrence issues, after the just culture principle, and each closes with a check or a recorded reason for none.
    Consequences
    The build fails if a trial is malformed, touches anything kept for a person, runs over 90 days or passes its end date, so no experiment is left running unowned (tests/test_automation.py Trials, with negative tests for each guardrail). The digest lists occurrences and trials. Nothing new can be merged, released or switched on without a person. The metaphor is a naming aid only: every term maps to a mechanism, and a term without one is dropped.

    ADR 0014: A global bar that fits a laptop screen Accepted

    Context
    With eleven items the bar folded About, Downloads and Estate into More below about 1,400 pixels, so most laptop users needed an extra click and could not see where About was.
    Decision
    What has changed and Strategy become one Context menu. Help leaves the bar: the masthead Help button leads to the user guide, which links the tutorials and roles, and it is marked when you are on those pages. Help stays in the site map and llms.txt.
    Consequences
    Nine items fit at 1,280 pixels with room to spare, checked in the browser at 1,280, 1,440 and 1,920 pixels (break: fold).

    ADR 0011: Name the profile DCAT-AP-UK Accepted

    Context
    National profiles of DCAT-AP are named DCAT-AP- and a country code (DCAT-AP-NO, DCAT-AP-SE, DCAT-AP-NL, DCAT-AP-DE). The draft was called DCAT-UK AP.
    Decision
    Call it DCAT-AP-UK, the DCAT Application Profile for the UK, in every page, file and namespace. Keep the dcatuk: prefix for terms.
    Consequences
    The w3id namespace becomes https://w3id.org/dcat-ap-uk/. The repository was renamed dcat-ap-uk on 6 November 2026, and the site moved with it.

    ADR 0012: Integrations through a conformance kit and one reference implementation Accepted

    Context
    Suppliers need to show conformance without reading the whole profile, and customers need to check claims.
    Decision
    Publish test records with expected verdicts, level badges, a command line, a local HTTP API and a GitHub Action, all running validator.js.
    Consequences
    A product conforms when it gives the expected verdict on every kit record. Product entries are background knowledge until each supplier confirms them (O-19).

    ADR 0013: Model contract clauses for open standards and exit Proposed

    Context
    Lock-in comes from proprietary formats, closed interfaces and missing exit terms. The Open Standards Principles and the Technology Code of Practice ask buyers to prevent it.
    Decision
    Offer clauses in seven groups (open standards, evidence, ownership, exit, change and debt, security, accessibility) as a downloadable file.
    Consequences
    They are a starting point, not legal advice. Commercial and legal review comes first (O-20).

    ADR 0006: A proposed w3id.org namespace Proposed

    Context
    Issue 13 asked for a persistent identifier.
    Decision
    Use https://w3id.org/dcat-ap-uk/ for the profile, its terms and codelists.
    Consequences
    Identifiers do not resolve until the redirect is merged at w3id.org (submitted as pull request 7100 on 6 November 2026).

    The case against, answered

    • This is just the xGov MEM with more fields; departments will not adopt it. Most of the change is fixes the xGov MEM's own users raised (20 open issues) and alignment that suppliers already implement for Europe. Records convert automatically. The base rate for voluntary metadata standards is low adoption without a mandate; the critical data asset switch ties it to a policy that already requires metadata.
    • Making modified optional weakens the record. A false date is worse than none (issue 30). The profile asks for it whenever known and forbids inventing it. Critical data assets still need created and time covered.
    • An unendorsed proposal in a personal repository should not be called DCAT-AP-UK. The name follows issue 78 and the MIWG discussion. Every page says it is proposed and not endorsed. If the owners prefer another name, it changes in one file.
    • Requiring DCAT-AP mandatory properties (access URL, endpoint URL) adds burden. Both are already held by any catalogue that publishes a file or an API. For internal APIs the guidance says to give a gateway address, not an internal host.

    Theory of change

    Problem
    Metadata about UK public sector data is recorded differently in every catalogue, so data is hard to find, judge and share, and returns are retyped.
    Activities
    • A profile with clear obligations and codelists
    • Shapes and schemas tools can check against
    • A portal that explains it to every role
    • A change process that keeps it current
    Outputs
    • Records that conform
    • Catalogues and tools that declare and test conformance
    • Converted xGov MEM records
    Outcomes
    • Data found across catalogues with one search
    • Fitness judged before asking
    • Returns built from catalogue records
    Impact
    Public sector data is found, trusted and reused, supporting a pan-UK public data infrastructure and the National Data Library.
    Assumptions
    • Catalogue owners will change exports if the cost is low and the benefit is clear
    • A mandate or endorsement follows a credible proposal
    We would be wrong if
    • If fewer than half of the catalogue managers in research can map their fields using the crosswalk, the profile or the crosswalk is too hard
    • If no catalogue declares conformance within two releases of endorsement, adoption needs a different route

    Research plan

    • Five to eight sessions across catalogue managers, data owners, engineers and policy leads, including at least two people who use assistive technology.
    • Tasks: find what is mandatory for a critical data asset; convert an xGov MEM record; build a record for your own dataset; explain to a colleague what changed.
    • A tree test of the global bar and hubs with 20 participants before the next release.
    • Recruitment through the Metadata Implementation Working Group and the cross-government data catalogues working group; consent script and no recordings kept beyond analysis.

    Measures of success:

    • Catalogues that declare conformance to the profile.
    • Share of harvested records that pass the shapes.
    • Critical data asset returns built from catalogue records rather than retyped.
    • Backlog items open longer than one release.

    Data protection and algorithmic transparency

    Data protection
    Screening only. The portal processes no personal data: no analytics, no cookies, no forms of its own. Display choices stay in the browser. The contact form is a GitHub issue form and is public, so it says not to include personal data. Records built in the browser never leave it. No DPIA is needed for the portal; adopters must assess their own catalogues.
    Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard
    Not needed. The portal and profile make no algorithmic decisions about people. The validator applies published rules and shows its reasons.
